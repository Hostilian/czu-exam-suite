What is a System?	A group of elements and relations between these elements. A system is an entity that maintains its existence through the mutual interaction of its parts, and is more than the sum of its parts.	system_theory
What is a Closed System?	A system that does not need to interact with the environment to maintain its existence. Examples: atoms, molecules.	system_theory
What is an Open System?	A system that must interact with the environment to maintain its existence. Examples: people, organizations.	system_theory
What is a Complex System?	A system composed of multiple interacting elements, where the behavior of the system as a whole cannot be explained solely by understanding the behavior of each of its individual parts.	system_theory
What are the 5 key components of a Complex System?	1. <b>Self-Organization</b> (structure without external control)<br>2. <b>Emergence</b> (whole cannot be predicted from parts)<br>3. <b>Nonlinearity</b> (small changes have disproportionate effects)<br>4. <b>Adaptation</b> (behaves in response to environment)<br>5. <b>Feedback Loops</b> (outputs routed back as inputs).	system_theory
What is Self-Organization in a complex system?	The ability of a complex system to organize its structure or behavior without external control or direction.	system_theory
What is Emergence in a complex system?	A phenomenon where the behavior of the system as a whole cannot be fully predicted or explained by the behavior of its individual parts.	system_theory
What is Nonlinearity in a complex system?	A characteristic where a small change in one part of the system can cause a disproportionately large (or small) effect on the system as a whole.	system_theory
What is a Model?	A simplified version of a real-world process or system, created to understand, analyze, and optimize it.	system_theory
Why do we create Models instead of experimenting on real systems?	Trial-and-error on the original object is often impossible (e.g. non-ethical social experiments), and modeling is cheaper, faster, and reproducible.	system_theory
What is the difference between Normative and Descriptive models?	<b>Normative models:</b> Describe what *should* be done (evaluation based on criteria).<br><b>Descriptive models:</b> Describe what is *actually* being done (no judgment or evaluation criteria).	system_theory
What is the difference between Static and Dynamic models?	<b>Static models:</b> Represent a system at a specific point in time (no time progression).<br><b>Dynamic models:</b> Account for changes over time (continuous or discrete time intervals).	system_theory
What is the difference between Deterministic and Stochastic models?	<b>Deterministic models:</b> Assume all variables are known and can be precisely determined.<br><b>Stochastic models:</b> Account for randomness and uncertainty (probability distributions).	system_theory
What is Hard Systems Thinking?	An approach that optimizes design using defined performance criteria and selects the alternative which best meets the need and is feasible. Relies on powerful quantitative techniques.	systems_thinking
What are the disadvantages of Hard Systems Thinking?	Requires professional experts; can focus too much on logical and technical aspects while ignoring personal, social, and political dynamics.	systems_thinking
What is Soft Systems Thinking?	An approach that improves a conceptual model using soft systems applications (e.g. Soft Systems Methodology) to gain an understanding of the problem and negotiate a course of action. Stays connected to the personal/social aspect.	systems_thinking
What is a Well-Structured Problem?	A problem where we know the procedures and methods of the solution, have all necessary information, or know how to obtain it. Example: operational control.	systems_thinking
Who developed Soft Systems Methodology (SSM)?	Developed by <b>Peter Checkland</b> in the late 1960s at the University of Lancaster (UK). It is a learning and meaning development tool.	ssm
What are the Seven Stages of Peter Checkland's SSM?	1. Stage 1: The Problem Situation (Unstructured)<br>2. Stage 2: Problem Situation Expressed (Rich Picture)<br>3. Stage 3: Root Definitions of Relevant Systems (CATWOE)<br>4. Stage 4: Conceptual Model<br>5. Stage 5: Comparison of Model and Reality<br>6. Stage 6: Desirable and Feasible Changes<br>7. Stage 7: Action to Improve	ssm
What elements must be included in a Stage 2 Rich Picture?	1. <b>Structures</b> (layout, departments)<br>2. <b>Processes</b> (flows)<br>3. <b>Climate</b> (feelings, work environment)<br>4. <b>People</b> (stakeholders)<br>5. <b>Issues</b> (complaints, concerns)<br>6. <b>Conflicts</b> (friction points)	ssm
What does the CATWOE analysis mnemonic stand for?	<b>C</b> - Customers (Beneficiaries/Victims)<br><b>A</b> - Actors<br><b>T</b> - Transformation (Input ➔ Output)<br><b>W</b> - Weltanschauung (Worldview)<br><b>O</b> - Owners<br><b>E</b> - Environmental Constraints	ssm
In CATWOE, what is the Weltanschauung (Worldview)?	The underlying belief system that makes the system's transformation meaningful, valid, and worthwhile.	ssm
In CATWOE, what represents the Transformation (T)?	The core process converting inputs to outputs (expressed in terms of Input ➔ Output).	ssm
In CATWOE, who are the Owners (O)?	Those who have the authority to start, change, or stop the system.	ssm
In CATWOE, what are the Environmental Constraints (E)?	External limitations or boundaries that the system must accept as given (e.g. regulations, budget limits).	ssm
What is a Conceptual Model (SSM Stage 4)?	A logical diagram of activities (using verbs in the imperative, aiming for <b>$7 \pm 2$</b> activities) necessary to carry out the transformation defined in the root definition.	ssm
In SSM Stage 6, what three analyses are used to check if changes are feasible?	1. <b>Owner Analysis</b> (who has authority to act)<br>2. <b>Social System Analysis</b> (roles, norms, values)<br>3. <b>Political Analysis</b> (how power is expressed)	ssm
What is Linear Programming (LP)?	A mathematical method to make the most of available resources to achieve a desired outcome (maximize or minimize a linear objective function) subject to constraints.	linear_programming
Where does the optimal solution to a Linear Programming problem always lie?	It always lies at one of the corner points (vertices) of the feasible region.	linear_programming
What is a Slack Variable?	A variable representing unused resources in a "less than or equal to" (<=) constraint.	linear_programming
What is a Surplus Variable?	A variable representing the excess over a minimum requirement in a "greater than or equal to" (>=) constraint.	linear_programming
What is Simplex Sensitivity Analysis?	The study of how changes in model parameters (coefficients of the objective function or RHS values of constraints) affect the optimal solution.	linear_programming
What is the Range of Optimality in Linear Programming?	The range of the objective function coefficients (prices/profits) where the current optimal corner point (quantities) remains unchanged.	linear_programming
If a price coefficient changes within its Range of Optimality, what happens to decision variables and profit?	Optimal decision variables (quantities) remain <b>exactly the same</b>, but the total profit changes by: <br>$$\Delta \text{Profit} = \Delta \text{Price} \times \text{Final Value of variable}$$	linear_programming
What is the Range of Feasibility in Linear Programming?	The range of Right-Hand Side (RHS) resource values where the current shadow prices remain valid.	linear_programming
What is a Shadow Price?	The rate of change in the objective function value (e.g. profit) per 1-unit increase in a constraint's resource (RHS value), valid within the feasibility range.	linear_programming
What is the Shadow Price of a non-binding constraint?	<b>Zero (0)</b>. If there is unused resource (Slack > 0), increasing it further does not increase the profit.	linear_programming
In Wassily Leontief Input-Output analysis, what is the Row Sums (Output) equation?	$$X_i = \sum_{j} z_{ij} + Y_i$$<br>Total output of sector i = Sum of intermediate deliveries + Final demand.	input_output
In Wassily Leontief Input-Output analysis, what is the Column Sums (Input) equation?	$$X_j = \sum_{i} z_{ij} + \text{Primary Inputs}_j$$<br>Total input of sector j = Sum of intermediate purchases + Primary inputs (Wages, Materials, EBIT).	input_output
What is a Technical Coefficient (a_ij) in I-O analysis?	The value of inputs from sector i needed to produce 1 unit of output in sector j. Defined as: <br>$$a_{ij} = \frac{z_{ij}}{X_j}$$	input_output
What is the Leontief Inverse Matrix (I - A)^-1?	Also called the <b>multiplier matrix</b>, it represents the total direct and indirect inputs required across all sectors of the economy to satisfy one unit of final demand.	input_output
What is the Markov Property?	<b>Memorylessness:</b> The probability distribution of the next state depends <b>only</b> on the current state and not on the sequence of past states.	markov_chains
What is the crucial rule for the row sums of a Markov Chain transition matrix P?	The sum of each row in the transition matrix P must equal exactly <b>1.0</b> (100%).	markov_chains
What is an Absorbing State in a Markov Chain?	A state that, once entered, cannot be left. Mathematically: $P_{ii} = 1.0$.	markov_chains
What is a Transient State in a Markov Chain?	A state where, once left, there is a positive probability that the system will never return back to it.	markov_chains
What is an Irreducible Markov Chain?	A Markov chain where the entire state space is a single communicating class, meaning you can eventually reach any state from any other state.	markov_chains
In Kendall's M/M/s queuing notation, what does each letter represent?	<b>First M:</b> Markovian arrival process (Poisson arrivals, exponential inter-arrival times).<br><b>Second M:</b> Markovian service times (exponential).<br><b>s:</b> Number of parallel servers.	queuing_theory
What is the M/M/1 Server Utilization (rho) formula and its stability condition?	$$\rho = \frac{\lambda}{\mu}$$<br>Where $\lambda$ is arrival rate, $\mu$ is service rate. For the queue to be stable, we must have $\rho < 1.0$ ($\lambda < \mu$).	queuing_theory
In a Vensim Stock-Flow Diagram, what is a Stock (Level / Box Variable)?	Represents accumulations or states. Its equation is: <br>`Stock = INTEG (Inflows - Outflows, Initial Value)`.	vensim
In a Vensim PLE exam, what is the point deduction for connecting a stock to a stock directly?	<b>-10 points</b>. A stock can only connect to another stock via a Flow (Rate) pipe. Information arrows cannot go directly to stocks.	vensim
How do you determine loop polarity in a CLD or Vensim model?	Count the number of negative ($o$ or $-$) links in the closed loop. If it is <b>even</b> (or zero), it is a <b>Reinforcing (R)</b> loop. If it is <b>odd</b>, it is a <b>Balancing (B)</b> loop.	vensim
What is the "Limits to Success / Growth" system archetype?	A reinforcing loop (R1) drives rapid growth, which eventually runs into a resource constraint, activating a balancing loop (B2) that slows and stops the growth.	system_archetypes
What is the "Tragedy of the Commons" system archetype?	Multiple individuals share a common resource and act in their own self-interest, leading to overexploitation and eventual destruction of the resource for everyone.	system_archetypes
What is the "Shifting the Burden" system archetype?	A quick, short-term "fix" (External Intervention) solves a symptom, but has delayed side effects that weaken the system's long-term capability to solve the root cause, creating dependence.	system_archetypes
What is the "Fixes that Fail" system archetype?	A quick fix is applied to solve a symptom, but it triggers a delayed unintended consequence that makes the problem symptom worse.	system_archetypes
What is the "Drifting Goals / Eroding Goals" system archetype?	When performance fails to meet a target, instead of correcting performance (balancing loop 1), the goal is lowered to match actual performance (balancing loop 2).	system_archetypes
What is the "Accidental Adversaries" system archetype?	Two partners in a mutually beneficial relationship take local actions to optimize their own performance, which, after a delay, unintendedly undercuts the success of the other.	system_archetypes
What is a Discrete Event Simulation (DES)?	Modeling a system where variables change state only at discrete, distinct points in time (events), such as customer arrivals.	simulation_theory
What is a Monte Carlo Simulation?	A simulation technique that uses repeated random sampling from probability distributions to model uncertainty and calculate probability distributions of outcomes.	simulation_theory
What is an Agent-Based Simulation (ABS)?	Models individual autonomous decision-making entities ("agents") that interact with each other and their environment, producing emergent system-level behavior.	simulation_theory
